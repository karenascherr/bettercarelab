Better CARE Lab

Better weight care for children, adults, and families

Meet the teamSee our projects

The Better CARE Lab at Duke University studies how to deliver whole-person weight-related health care and how to connect children, adults, and families with the support they need. Led by Dr. Karen Scherr, a family medicine and obesity medicine physician, our team works with primary care clinics, health systems, and community partners such as the YMCA to make evidence-based care easier for families to find and to stay with.

What CARE stands for

The four principles below guide our work, and they grow out of our belief that every person has inherent worth and deserves to be treated with dignity at any body size, and that health is about much more than weight. Research on weight stigma shows how often people with obesity are denied that dignity in health care, and our projects are designed to change that.

Compassion

We design care that recognizes how much people with obesity have already been through, including the blame and bias many have experienced in health care.

Access

We work to connect families with treatment by building referral pathways between clinics and community programs and by designing treatment tools that fit into the way clinics already work.

Respect

We listen to patients, parents, and clinicians and use what they tell us to shape how care is offered and discussed.

Evidence

We use current clinical guidelines and rigorous research methods so that the care we help deliver is grounded in the best available science.

About the Lab

The Better CARE Lab is a research group in the Department of Family Medicine and Community Health at Duke University and part of the Duke Center for Childhood Obesity Research. We study how to deliver whole-person care for obesity and how to connect children, adults, and families with treatment that works for them.

Our approach

We start from the view that obesity is a chronic disease with many causes, and that people deserve the same thoughtful, evidence-based care for it that they would receive for any other health condition. Much of that care is already described in clinical guidelines, but many patients and families never receive it. Clinicians often lack the time and tools to offer it, community programs can be hard to find, and past experiences of weight stigma make some people reluctant to ask for help. Our research focuses on closing those gaps in primary care clinics, in pediatric weight management, and in the community.

How we work

Our projects draw on surveys, interviews, and data from the electronic health record, and we often combine these methods to understand both what is happening and why. We listen closely to patients, parents, and clinicians, and we use what they tell us to design tools and programs that fit the way clinics and families already work. We believe research is most useful when it is done in collaboration with the people and organizations who deliver care, and we value our partnerships with organizations such as the YMCA of the Triangle, the American Diabetes Association, and the Duke Healthy Lifestyles clinic.

Students and trainees

Students and trainees at every stage, from undergraduates to residents and fellows, take part in our research, and many have presented their work at national meetings and coauthored papers with the lab. Our Training page describes what working with the lab looks like at each level.

Karen Scherr is a board-certified family medicine and obesity medicine physician and an Assistant Professor in the Department of Family Medicine and Community Health at Duke University, where she directs the Better CARE Lab. She also serves as Director for Education and Training for the Duke Center for Childhood Obesity Research. She cared for children and adults in primary care for 8 years prior to shifting her clinical focus to pediatric weight management in 2026 as part of the Duke Healthy Lifestyles clinic. Her research focuses on improving how obesity treatment is delivered and how families are connected to it.

Dr. Scherr's interest in this field grew out of her own and her family's experiences with obesity and eating disorders. Those experiences showed her how much it matters to be treated with understanding when asking for help, and she tries never to lose sight of what it is like to be the patient. She believes good obesity care looks at the whole person, including their mental health and their family life. That view shapes both the care she gives her own patients and the questions her lab chooses to study.

Dr. Scherr earned her MD and PhD at Duke through the Medical Scientist Training Program. Her PhD is in Business Administration (Marketing and Consumer Behavior) from Duke's Fuqua School of Business, and her doctoral and early research examined how physicians and patients communicate and make treatment decisions together. That background gives her an interdisciplinary view of the many people and systems that shape health. She then completed her family medicine residency at Duke, where she served as chief resident, followed by the National Clinician Scholars Program fellowship at Duke.

Training

Students and trainees are part of nearly every project in the Better CARE Lab, and many of them have presented their work at national meetings and coauthored papers with Dr. Scherr. The sections below describe what working with the lab looks like at each stage of training, so that students can decide whether the lab is a good fit before an opening is posted.

Current openings

Undergraduate students

Undergraduates in our lab work as members of the research team on our projects in obesity treatment and diabetes prevention. Depending on the project, they may help review research literature, prepare surveys and interview guides, recruit and schedule participants, manage and clean data, and code interview transcripts with the team. Students who stay with the lab for several semesters take on more responsibility over time, and some go on to present a poster or contribute to a paper. We give priority to students who can commit to at least two semesters and 10 hours per week. Coursework in research methods or statistics is helpful, and an interest in health care, nutrition, psychology, or public health is a good match for our work.

Medical students

Medical students usually take on a defined piece of a larger project, such as an analysis of existing data or a set of interviews with patients, parents, or clinicians, with the goal of presenting at a national meeting and writing a paper. Our projects connect closely to clinical care, so they are a good fit for students who are considering family medicine, pediatrics, internal medicine, or obesity medicine. Students can work with the lab during the third-year research year (Dr. Scherr is an approved third-year mentor), and she is also open to working with students in earlier years if it is the right fit.

Graduate students

We work with master's and PhD students, including students in Population Health Sciences, on projects that fit their training and career goals. Graduate students often lead analyses and manuscripts, and some use lab data for a thesis, practicum, or dissertation chapter. Several members of our team first joined the lab as graduate students.

Residents and fellows

Residents and fellows can work with Dr. Scherr on a scholarly project that fits within the research time in their training program. The best projects are ones with clear objectives that can fit into the busy life of a clinical trainee.

Our Projects

Our projects look for practical ways to help people with obesity get effective, respectful treatment. Some focus on the tools clinicians use in primary care, and others focus on partnerships between health systems and community organizations that make it easier for families to find support close to home.

Current and recent projects

Healthy Teens: Treating adolescent obesity in primary care

Most teens with obesity are seen in primary care, but many clinicians have limited time and few tools to offer the treatment recommended in the 2023 American Academy of Pediatrics clinical practice guideline. In this project, we are developing and testing tools that fit into existing clinic workflows and support clinicians in offering evidence-based obesity treatment to adolescents, with the goal of preventing type 2 diabetes.

FundedAmerican Diabetes Association, 2025 to 2028.

Clinic-community partnerships to improve evidence-based treatment of obesity in children and adults

Dr. Scherr has worked with the YMCA of the Triangle since 2019 to connect patients from Duke clinics with the YMCA's evidence-based programs, and in 2026 this partnership became a funded project. The project covers two YMCA programs: Healthy Weight and Your Child, a family-based program that helps children and parents build healthier routines together, and the Diabetes Prevention Program, which helps adults with prediabetes lower their risk of type 2 diabetes. Many children and adults who could benefit from these programs never hear about them, so we are studying how to connect patients from Duke clinics to the YMCA and how to support them once they enroll.

FundedYMCA of the Triangle, 2026 to 2027.

Diabetes Prevention Alliance

The National Diabetes Prevention Program helps adults with prediabetes lower their risk of type 2 diabetes, but it is underused, especially in rural and underserved areas. Dr. Scherr is an investigator on this American Diabetes Association project, along with Dr. Anastasia Alexopoulos, which is working to scale the program in underserved communities in North Carolina.

FundedAmerican Diabetes Association, 2024 to 2027.

What parents think about pediatric obesity treatment

We surveyed and interviewed parents about the 2023 clinical practice guideline for pediatric obesity to learn which recommendations they find acceptable and what concerns they have about treatment options such as medications and surgery. This work helps clinicians prepare for conversations with families about treatment. Because of the public interest in this work, Dr. Scherr was interviewed on the WRAL morning news and the WPTF morning radio show to talk with parents in the Triangle about what the new guideline means for their families.

PublishedPediatric Obesity, 2026. In the newsWRAL and WPTF

Linking children and caregivers in the health record

Obesity often runs in families, and treating a child is easier when the whole household is supported. Led by Dr. Lilianna Suarez with Dr. Scherr as primary mentor, this project examines how to link children in obesity treatment with their caregivers in the electronic health record so that care teams can support parents and children together.

PresentedObesityWeek 2025.

How adolescents experience weight stigma in health care

Most of what we know about weight stigma in pediatric health care comes from parents and clinicians, and we rarely hear from teens themselves. In this study, adolescents receiving care at the Duke Healthy Lifestyles Clinic completed a survey about stigmatizing experiences in health care, and a subset took part in interviews about those experiences. Our goal was to learn how teens themselves experience weight stigma in health care and how it affects them, so that clinician training and care can be designed around what teens tell us. The study was led by Brooke Wagner with Dr. Scherr as a mentor and senior author.

In reviewUnder revision at Childhood Obesity.

Electronic referrals to diabetes prevention programs

Dr. Scherr led the development of a bidirectional electronic referral between a health system and the YMCA's Diabetes Prevention Program, which made it possible for clinicians to refer patients with prediabetes directly from the electronic health record. A related study with the University of Michigan showed how a direct referral order can connect patients to community programs.

PublishedAnnals of Family Medicine, 2025.
Earlier work

How physicians and patients talk about treatment

Dr. Scherr's earlier research used recordings of real clinical appointments to study how physicians communicate with patients about serious treatment decisions. She developed the Physician Recommendation Coding System to measure how strongly physicians recommend a treatment, and her studies of men with early-stage prostate cancer showed that physician recommendations often carried more weight than patients' own preferences. Related work looked at how urologists described robotic surgery depending on whether their hospital offered it and how conversations about cost affect the relationship between patients and physicians.

Selected publicationsMedical Decision Making 2017 (two papers); Health Communication 2017 and 2019; American Journal of Bioethics 2017; Journal of Behavioral Medicine 2021

Shared decision making and patient engagement

Dr. Scherr has also studied how to help patients take a more active role in their medical decisions. This work includes a training video that prepared men with prostate cancer to take part in their appointments, a comparison of decision aids for patients with different levels of health literacy, and studies of how the words physicians use, such as "cure" or "clinical remission," shape the choices people living with HIV make about treatment and research.

Selected publicationsMedical Decision Making 2022; Patient Education and Counseling 2020; AIDS and Behavior 2020; Journal of Virus Eradication 2019; Association for Consumer Research Conference 2015 and 2016

Consumer behavior and health choices

Her doctoral training in marketing and consumer behavior led to studies of everyday health decisions made outside the clinic, including how people judge the healthiness of food portions and how the design of HealthCare.gov affected people's understanding of their insurance options. This background still shapes how the lab thinks about the many people and systems that influence whether families get care.

Selected publicationsManagement Science 2019; Medical Decision Making Policy & Practice 2017

Support the Better CARE Lab

Gifts to the Better CARE Lab let us do more than our grants alone can pay for. Research grants usually cover a specific study for a set period, and philanthropic support allows us to reach more families than the original grant allowed and to keep successful programs going after the funding period ends. Gifts also help us start new ideas and support the students and trainees who work with us.

Your gift may support

  • Research staff time, so that funded projects can reach more families and community sites than the original budget allowed
  • Paid research positions for undergraduates and other trainees, especially so that students who cannot afford to volunteer can gain research experience
  • New studies that test ways to connect children and families with obesity treatment
  • Travel for trainees to present their work at national meetings such as ObesityWeek
  • Community events and materials developed with partners such as the YMCA of the Triangle
